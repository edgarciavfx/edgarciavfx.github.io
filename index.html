What I'm doing now

Business analysis, then the software the analysis calls for

Since July 2026 I've been consulting independently for small and mid-sized businesses in Jalisco: going into the real operation, mapping the process as it actually runs, crossing the numbers, and then building what the findings point at.

On a recent engagement with a medical-apparel manufacturer, I consolidated raw sales, invoicing and accounting exports into a single analytical view. The headline finding wasn't in any of their reports:

99days of DSO, measured — never previously calculated
~50%of invoiced revenue sitting uncollected
330data anomalies across disconnected systems
94spellings of one customer's name, making per-client margin uncomputable

The finding I'm proudest of took reading formulas rather than numbers: a current-year P&L that reconciled perfectly against its own parts while its revenue row summed last year's workbook. It surfaced only because the analysis crossed the books against a source the client doesn't type by hand — their stamped tax receipts. The readers that made that possible are open source as mxbooks.

I also mapped the full order-to-invoice flow in per-role swimlanes, reconciled real per-product margin against the published price list, and scoped a mobile-first inventory system built around how the operations lead actually works on the floor.

mxbooks

Library

Dependency-free readers for the three formats a Mexican business's books arrive in: spreadsheets, stamped CFDI 4.0 receipts, and SAT portal reports. Keeps what normal parsers discard — formula text beside cached values, the stamp UUID rather than the first UUID in the file. 39 tests, CI on Python 3.9–3.13.
